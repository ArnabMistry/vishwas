>   - **Spatial Regridding:** Nearest-neighbor interpolation using xarray onto the IMD grid.
>   - **ML Error Regressor:** XGBoost Regressor (n_estimators=100, max_depth=5, learning_rate=0.1, hist tree method) trained on 7 synoptic and spatial features (f_apcp_24h, f_cape, f_hgt_500, f_u_850, f_v_850, lat, lon).
>   - **Uncertainty Intervals:** Split Conformal Prediction via MAPIE SplitConformalRegressor (nominal 80% coverage; empirical test-set coverage 90.99% under exchangeability). Artifact and schema keys (cqr_model.pkl, cqr_lower, cqr_upper, cqr_bounds) are legacy compatibility identifiers representing split conformal prediction intervals, not Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR).
>   - **Explainability:** TreeSHAP (shap.TreeExplainer) local feature attribution with domain-specific model-attributed linguistic descriptions (not causal meteorological claims).
>   - **Risk Metrics:** bust_risk_score is a derived model-based risk indicator in [0, 1] (not a calibrated probability); bust_prob is a legacy compatibility alias; fci is a Forecast Confidence Index derived inversely from conformal prediction interval width.

Master Product and Engineering Specification: AI-Based Forecast Bust DetectionPart 1: Understand the Problem at Its CoreThe operational challenge presented by the Ministry of Earth Sciences (MoES) and the National Centre for Medium Range Weather Forecasting (NCMRWF) addresses a profound vulnerability in modern meteorology: the sporadic, catastrophic failure of numerical models during high-impact weather events. The actual problem is not that Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP) models are generally inaccurate; it is that their medium-range (Days 1–10) accuracy degrades non-linearly and unpredictably during rapidly evolving atmospheric systems. These forecast failures, known as "forecast busts," severely compromise operational decision-making, leading to either unissued early warnings or costly false alarms that erode public trust.The underlying scientific problem stems from the chaotic nature of the atmosphere and the limitations of atmospheric modeling. NWP models, such as the NCMRWF Unified Model (NCUM-G) which runs at a 12 km horizontal resolution with 70 vertical levels, rely on physical parameterizations to resolve sub-grid scale processes like convection, cloud microphysics, and boundary layer turbulence. During calm periods, these approximations hold. However, during rapid regime transitions, microscopic uncertainties in initial conditions interact with parameterized approximations, leading to upscale error growth. A forecast bust occurs when this error growth causes a sudden, significant drop in predictive skill, typically quantified by a precipitous decline in the Anomaly Correlation Coefficient (ACC) or a spike in Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for key parameters like the 500 hPa geopotential height.Medium-range forecasts (Days 1–10) occupy a uniquely difficult temporal domain. While short-range forecasts (0–3 days) are heavily constrained by initial conditions (data assimilation) and extended-range forecasts (weeks 2–4) rely on slowly evolving boundary conditions like Sea Surface Temperatures, the medium range is governed by the chaotic internal dynamics of the atmosphere. Small initial errors in Rossby wave phasing or latent heat release can amplify massively by Day 5.Rapidly evolving weather systems exacerbate these physics limitations. Monsoon depressions, which are low-pressure areas forming primarily over the Bay of Bengal, drive the bulk of the Indian Summer Monsoon Rainfall. Their dynamics rely on complex barotropic and baroclinic instabilities, along with intense latent heat release that models often struggle to resolve temporally, leading to severe spatial displacement errors in the forecast track. Similarly, heavy rainfall events are highly localized and driven by mesoscale convective systems that fall below the effective resolution of global models. Western Disturbances (WDs) are extratropical upper-tropospheric troughs that interact violently with the Himalayan orography. When NWP models fail to accurately simulate the interaction between these upper-level Rossby wave anomalies and lower-level moisture transport from the Arabian Sea, the result is massive precipitation forecast busts over northern India. Tropical cyclones present another domain of rapid evolution, where Tropical Cyclone Heat Potential (TCHP) and internal diabatic heating drive rapid intensification that global models frequently underestimate or displace. Finally, heat waves and active/break monsoon phases represent large-scale regime transitions driven by subsidence and planetary wave propagation; missing the onset of these phases results in prolonged, multi-day forecast failures.The meteorological variables most deeply associated with these failures include the 500 hPa geopotential height (indicating synoptic wave patterns), 850 hPa winds (indicating low-level moisture transport), Convective Available Potential Energy (CAPE), upper-level divergence, and total column water vapor.It is vital to draw a rigid distinction between forecast uncertainty and forecast error. Forecast uncertainty represents the theoretical spread of possible atmospheric states, traditionally estimated using Ensemble Prediction Systems (EPS) like the NCMRWF Global Ensemble Prediction System (NEPS-G). Forecast error, conversely, is the actual divergence between the deterministic forecast and the realized observation. The central product principle of this architecture is built on the realization that high uncertainty does not inherently mean high error, and low uncertainty does not guarantee a correct forecast. Often, the most dangerous forecast busts occur when the ensemble spread is low (the model is highly confident) but the entire ensemble is systematically displaced from reality due to a shared physical parameterization flaw. Therefore, predicting weather and predicting model reliability are fundamentally different tasks. We are not trying to predict the rainfall; we are trying to predict the mathematical probability that the NCUM-G model's rainfall prediction will be wildly incorrect.Part 2: Research the Real Scientific LandscapeThe scientific landscape surrounding forecast verification and bust detection is highly mature but heavily siloed within academic and operational institutions. Organizations such as ECMWF, NCMRWF, NOAA, and WMO continuously evaluate forecast reliability using established statistical paradigms. The ECMWF, for instance, utilizes the spread-error relationship to assess ensemble reliability, diagnosing situations where atmospheric blocking or anomalous latent heat release triggers widespread forecast busts over Europe. The NCMRWF conducts rigorous spatial verification of its NCUM-G and NEPS models using the Contiguous Rain Area (CRA) method, which decomposes total forecast error into volume, pattern, and spatial displacement errors.Standard approaches for uncertainty quantification involve reliability diagrams, the Brier Score for probabilistic forecasts, and the Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS). For spatial precipitation verification, the Fractions Skill Score (FSS) has become the gold standard, as it overcomes the "double-penalty" problem inherent in high-resolution models by comparing the fractional coverage of events within expanding spatial neighborhoods rather than demanding exact point-to-point matches. Furthermore, object-based diagnostic evaluation (MODE) is utilized to identify and track coherent meteorological objects, analyzing centroid displacement and structural decay.In the realm of Machine Learning, researchers are increasingly applying gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM) and deep learning (ConvLSTMs) to post-process and calibrate NWP outputs. Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR) is emerging as a state-of-the-art methodology for generating statistically guaranteed prediction intervals, replacing traditional, uncalibrated variance estimates.Given this sophisticated landscape, a generic hackathon submission will be easily identifiable by its lack of atmospheric physics integration. Hundreds of teams will likely download a random historical weather CSV, train a basic Random Forest or generic LSTM to predict a binary "error" flag, and display the result on a rounded, gradient-heavy web dashboard with an arbitrary percentage dial labeled "AI Confidence: 75%." Such generic submissions will ignore spatial error covariance, fail to utilize ensemble spread metrics, treat weather as a standard tabular dataset rather than a dynamic fluid system, and present a chatbot attempting to explain meteorological phenomena without any grounding in the actual model physics. These baseline ideas represent the exact antithesis of a credible, government-grade operational tool.Part 3: Find the White SpaceTo captivate domain experts and hackathon judges, the prototype must transcend standard point-based regression and embrace spatial verification, regime awareness, and rigorous uncertainty quantification. The innovation must emerge from understanding that NWP errors propagate as spatial waves and are heavily dependent on synoptic regimes. Key inputs include gridded fields of Precipitation, 2m Temperature, 500 hPa Geopotential Height, 850 hPa Winds, and Convective Available Potential Energy (CAPE).Meteorological Feature Extraction: Raw variables are transformed into indicators of atmospheric stress. Instead, it predicts the continuous error utilizing Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR).Let $X$ represent the vector of meteorological features at grid point $x$:$$X = [F_{precip}, \sigma_{precip}, F_{CAPE}, \vert{}\nabla F_{Z500}\vert{}, \text{WindShear}, L]$$Using MAPIE, we fit conditional quantile estimators to obtain the lower and upper bounds of the prediction interval for a miscoverage rate $\alpha$ (e.g., $\alpha = 0.2$ for 80% confidence):$$\hat{C}(X) = [\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(X) - \hat{s}, \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(X) + \hat{s}]$$
where $\hat{s}$ is the conformal calibration conformity score.Definitions:Forecast Bust Probability (FBP): The probability that the true error exceeds a threshold $\tau$. If assuming a split-normal approximation between the quantiles, we calculate $P(Y > \tau \vert{} X)$.Forecast Confidence Indicator (FCI): A normalized metric (0-100) inversely proportional to the width of the conformal interval. Wide intervals indicate high uncertainty.$$FCI = 100 \times \exp(-\lambda (\hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2} - \hat{q}_{\alpha/2}))$$where $\lambda$ is a scaling constant tuned to the climatological variance of the variable.Part 8: Model SelectionEvaluating realistic ML approaches for spatial error prediction:Deep Learning (ConvLSTM / ST-GCN): Scientifically sound for spatiotemporal sequence modeling.
